Why this note exists§

Spoke D evaluates governance labor, compensation, treasury funding, and capture through the lens of reciprocity. Until now the spoke’s reliance on reciprocity has been normative and structural: reciprocity loops are how the commons sustains itself without enclosing itself. This note anchors a new and independent kind of support — experimental psychology — for a claim the framework has carried implicitly: reciprocity is not a default human expectation; it is the exchange logic of relationships perceived as equal. Where the relationship is perceived as hierarchical, a different logic takes over — precedent — and that substitution is the cognitive substrate of several capture pathways the spoke is built to detect.

The note records what the source establishes, fixes its position in the architecture, traces its implications for Spoke D’s core questions, and attaches a proposed instrument. It is deliberately spoke-local. Hub feedback criteria are listed at the end; none are yet met.

What the source establishes§

Chen and Saxe (2026) report six preregistered experiments (total N = 599, U.S. adults) combining third-party vignette judgments with first-person incentivized coordination games. The findings, in the order of their load-bearing relevance here:

Reciprocity expectations are specific to equal relationships. When a relationship was described as symmetric — or labeled with archetypal equal roles like cousins or coworkers — a single generous act created the expectation that the recipient would be generous next. This is the classic turn-taking result, and it appeared only under equality cues.

Hierarchy cues activate a precedent logic instead. When the relationship was described as asymmetric — abstractly (“more power, status or influence”) or concretely (boss–employee, uncle–nephew) — the same generous act created the opposite expectation: that the same actor would be generous again. No debt is registered; a role is.

The effect holds in both directions of the hierarchy. Whether the generous actor was higher- or lower-ranked made no difference (Bayes factors gave strong evidence for the null). Status asymmetry itself activates the precedent norm, irrespective of who gives.

The cue required is minimal. A single sentence of relational description flipped the entire exchange logic — overriding the turn-taking default that decades of stranger-paradigm economics treated as fundamental. The authors note that the predominance of reciprocity in lab games likely reflects an implicit assumption of equal rank among strangers, an assumption that is “brittle.”

Precedent-following operates as an abstract rule, not just a cultural script. Study 4 dissociated scenario-specific priors from an abstract repeat-your-role rule: even when the observed first act contradicted participants’ prior beliefs about who should give, hierarchy cues still produced expectations of repetition. Both mechanisms contribute independently.

People follow precedent at personal cost. In the incentivized Leader–Follower game (Study 6), participants who had taken the generous (lower-payoff) role with a hierarchical partner continued taking it — repeatedly accepting the disadvantaged position. The expectation is strong enough to govern costly first-person behavior, not just third-party prediction.

The entrenchment is individually rational. The authors note (citing O’Connor) that following an asymmetric precedent is often in every individual’s strategic best interest — including the repeatedly disadvantaged party — because it minimizes miscoordination risk, even as it sustains and entrenches unequal payoffs.

The paper is also the first direct experimental test of Graeber’s speculation in Debt that balanced reciprocity is the exchange logic of actual or potential equals specifically, and that hierarchical relationships run instead on precedent. The speculation survived.

Position in the architecture§

This source enters as supporting mechanism, not derivational premise. The rights derivation is normative and does not rest on it; nothing in the chain from money ontology to amendment design acquires or loses validity if the psychology turns out to be more complicated. What the source supplies is descriptive: an account of what expectations relational structure produces in human participants. That is exactly the shape of input the instruments layer requires — sensors are descriptive devices in service of normative invariants — and it is why this note attaches an instrument proposal rather than a derivation amendment.

Two inferential-distance caveats govern all use of this source:

  1. Scope of the evidence. The experiments concern dyadic interpersonal generosity among U.S. adults. Extension to participant–institution relationships in a blockchain polity is analogical. The direction of the analogy is plausible — institutions are routinely cognized through relational schemas — but every claim below that crosses from dyad to polity should be read as mechanism-candidate, flagged for possible future validation in governance-flavored paradigms.
  2. Descriptive, not normative. The findings tell us what expectations people form, not what rights they hold. The framework’s normative content is untouched. The findings matter because the enforcement environment of rights — whether violations are noticed, registered as debts, and expected to be corrected — is partly constituted by these expectations.

Implications for Spoke D’s core questions§

When does volunteerism become extraction? — a mechanism§

The spoke has asked this as a normative boundary question. The source supplies a candidate causal mechanism for how the boundary gets crossed without anyone perceiving it:

The moment the relationship between a contributor and the ecosystem is perceived as asymmetric, a single act of governance labor stops generating an expectation of return and starts generating an expectation of repetition. The volunteer DRep, reviewer, or facilitator who does the work once is expected — by observers and, per Study 6, plausibly by themselves — to do it again. No debt is registered anywhere in the system. Precedent logic suppresses the bookkeeping that reciprocity logic performs.

This explains a pattern the spoke has treated as a risk but not mechanized: volunteer governance labor entrenches silently. The extraction is invisible not because anyone hides it but because the cognitive regime in force does not record it as a debt. This is a relational-layer instance of the invisibilization dynamic the rights articulation identifies at the settlement layer: the violation’s invisibility is its means of sustaining itself.

When is compensation legitimate? — a reframing§

The spoke’s compensation question has been framed as fairness: is the payment proportionate to the service, accountable to the commons, non-capturing? The source adds a second function that may be the more constitutionally important one:

Compensation is a regime-keeping device. Explicit, accounted-for payment keeps the contributor–commons relationship inside the reciprocity regime — the regime in which imbalances remain visible, are registered as debts, and are expected to be corrected. Uncompensated labor in a perceived-asymmetric relationship drifts into the precedent regime, where the same questions cannot even arise because the bookkeeping has stopped.

This cuts against a common intuition in commons discourse that volunteerism is the purer form and compensation the corruption risk. Compensation carries capture risks the spoke already tracks; but non-compensation carries a regime risk the spoke has not tracked: it converts contributors into role-holders whose continued giving is expected rather than owed. The legitimacy question should therefore be asked in both directions — not only “does this payment capture?” but “does the absence of payment entrench?”

When does governance support become capture? — acquiescence via reframing§

The most operationally significant finding is the minimality of the cue. If one sentence of relational description flips the exchange logic, then the ecosystem’s self-description is a causal governance variable, not rhetoric.

The constitutive framing at the center of the hub — token holders as co-constituents of the commons, not users, customers, or subjects of institutions — is, in this light, the framing condition under which participants apply reciprocity norms to governance actors at all. Under co-constituent framing, an institutional taking registers as a debt and generates an expectation of return or correction. Under hierarchical framing (ordinary holders vs. founding entities, delegators vs. DReps, community vs. Constitutional Committee), the same taking registers as precedent: institutions are expected to keep doing what they do, and participants are expected to keep deferring — including, per Study 6, at recognized personal cost.

That expectation shift is capture acquiescence in psychological form, and it can be produced entirely through relational reframing, upstream of any procedural change. A capture pathway therefore exists that no procedural audit can see: convert the perceived relationship from symmetric to hierarchical, and subsequent extraction is pre-normalized — each act individually rational to accept, collectively regressive, never tripping an alarm because the alarm (reciprocity bookkeeping) has been switched off at the relational layer.

Cross-spoke note: the Trajectory Framework’s Precedent Assessment§

The Trajectory Analysis Framework already treats precedent as the unit of constitutional drift (“if this action’s logic were applied to the next ten similar situations…”). The source strengthens this with cognitive grounding: precedent is not merely a legal or procedural phenomenon but an abstract rule in observers’ heads, activated by hierarchy cues, strong enough to override their own prior beliefs about who should act.

The consequence for assessment practice: a single governance act in a perceived-asymmetric context generates an expectation field, and that field is part of the act’s constitutional cost. Snapshot evaluation cannot capture it; trajectory evaluation can, but only if the precedent assessment asks not just “what does this permit?” but “what does this lead observers to expect, and under which relational framing was it observed?” The O’Connor point closes the loop with the framework’s named failure mode: precedent-following is individually defensible for every participant, including the disadvantaged, and collectively regressive — drift that no individual decision flags.

Proposed instrument: the reciprocity-expectation sensor§

For the standing sensor batteries attached to rights patterns (pilot: SETTLEMENT THAT CANNOT BE CONDITIONED), this source motivates a distal sensor of the kind the framework has argued is superior to crisis-driven reorientation.

What it senses. The decay of reciprocity expectation toward institutional governance actors — the leading indicator that the participant–institution relationship has been cognitively re-filed from symmetric (co-constituent) to hierarchical (subject), which precedes and enables enclosure.

Why it is distal. Every test in the rights articulation fires on a violation or its structural preconditions. This sensor fires on the relational regime shift that makes violations cheap — before any taking occurs. It converts what would otherwise be near-death learning (discovering, mid-capture, that the community no longer expects correction) into a cheap early correction (noticing the framing drift while it is still only framing).

Candidate observables (to be refined; listed here as a starting battery):

  • Debt-registration discourse. When institutional actors take resources, expand scope, or impose costs, does community discourse register a debt — language of owing, returning, correcting, accounting — or does it register a role (“that’s what they do”)? The shift from debt-language to role-language is the regime transition in linguistic form.
  • Correction expectation. After an acknowledged institutional overreach, do participants articulate an expectation of restitution or rebalancing, or only of disclosure and continuation? Precedent regimes produce the latter.
  • Volunteerism framing. Is sustained uncompensated governance labor discussed as an outstanding imbalance the commons should resolve, or as a settled feature of the contributor’s identity? The second framing indicates the bookkeeping has stopped.
  • Self-description drift. Frequency and placement of hierarchical relational vocabulary (users, community-vs-institution constructions, deference idioms) versus constitutive vocabulary (co-constituents, participants, members) in official communications, proposals, and rationales. Per the source, the cue dose required for regime shift is small; the sensor’s sensitivity should match.
  • Direct probe (optional, higher-cost). The source’s vignette paradigm adapts naturally: present participants with a governance-flavored generous/extractive first act under the ecosystem’s actual relational descriptions and measure whether they predict reciprocation or repetition. This would simultaneously validate the dyad-to-polity analogy and calibrate the cheaper discourse sensors.

Interpretation rule. The sensor does not detect violations and must not be read as one. A precedent-regime reading is a signal that the enforcement environment of the rights is degrading — that violations, when they come, will be pre-normalized. Its output routes to the trajectory log and to the relational-framing question in precedent assessment, not to the violation tests.

Candidate pattern (parked)§

The material may eventually earn a pattern in the Alexandrian language — working name RECIPROCITY PRESUPPOSES EQUALITY — whose inner polarity would be the tension between hierarchy’s genuine coordination value (precedent is cheap, predictable, and miscoordination-minimizing; the source is clear-eyed about this) and the commons’ need to keep institutional relationships inside the regime where imbalance stays visible. The Therefore would point at the maintenance of symmetric relational framing as a precondition for every reciprocity loop Spoke D depends on. Parked until the sensor work clarifies whether this is a pattern in its own right or an instruments-section component of existing rights patterns.

Hub feedback criteria§

This note should remain spoke-local unless one of the following obtains:

  1. The direct-probe validation (or equivalent evidence) confirms the dyad-to-polity analogy, at which point the capture theory in the hub should incorporate relational-regime shift as a named capture pathway.
  2. The compensation-as-regime-keeping reframing survives application to real treasury and DRep-incentive cases, at which point the hub’s reciprocity-loop account should distinguish regime maintenance from fairness as separate legitimacy functions of compensation.
  3. The candidate pattern is drafted and stresses the template in a way that reveals a missing element in the pattern language’s treatment of relational preconditions.

Open questions§

  • Does the both-directions finding (precedent regardless of who gives) hold for institutional generosity downward — e.g., foundation grants, treasury disbursements — where the framework would want recipients to retain reciprocal standing rather than settle into beneficiary roles?
  • How does the sensor distinguish legitimate role differentiation (some asymmetry is functional and consented-to within bounded scope) from regime drift? The bounded-consent analysis in the transaction work is the likely resource here.
  • Cross-cultural limits: the source samples U.S. adults; the ecosystem is global. The authors speculate the hierarchy→precedent link is shared across societies while scripts vary, but the sensor’s discourse observables will need locale-aware calibration.
